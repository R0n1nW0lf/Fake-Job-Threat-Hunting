# Correlation Analysis

This analysis compares the fifteen investigated cases to identify shared indicators, reused material, and common patterns. It separates what the evidence shows from what it does not.

## Shared patterns

### Reused templates and sister postings

- CASE-002: a sibling posting ("Operations Analyst", New York, LinkedIn 4478159278) ran under the same company name using an identical template.
- CASE-003: the same company page ran a second template posting ("IT Service Desk Analyst") with a different, also unrelated, poster.
- CASE-005: a template-identical sister posting ("Data Insights Analyst (Entry / Junior)", New York, 2026-09-07) ran under the same company name.
- CASE-006: the description was a generic template naming no employer at all, and exact-phrase searches for its distinctive lines returned zero results anywhere.
- CASE-001: the posting text was copied from a real employer's job (EaglePicher Technologies), and later reposts under the same name copied other companies' text (Telos in the 2026-10-04 repost; a MANTECH role in the 2026-10-09 "Senior Cyber Security Analyst" repost).

### Company-page impersonation and placeholder display names

- CASE-006 is the clearest example of an identity inconsistency: a real Mexican law firm's LinkedIn page displays the placeholder name "No aplica", while its URL slug still names the firm. A second, unrelated company's page (slug "catelca", Panama) displays the same placeholder name. **Whether either page was renamed, by whom, or without authorization is Not Verified** — the confirmed observation is the placeholder display and the name/slug mismatch, not the mechanism behind it.
- CASE-002 and CASE-005 used the names of real foreign organisations (a Dutch coalition; a Costa Rican outsourcing firm) that have no connection to the advertised roles.
- CASE-003's page belongs to a real but unrelated Dutch micro-business.

### Poster affiliation mismatches

- CASE-002: poster publicly a T-Mobile program manager; no affiliation with NL AAA.
- CASE-003: poster an unrelated person in Argentina, in an unrelated profession; a second posting on the same page used yet another unrelated poster.
- CASE-005: poster publicly a Tata Consultancy Services support analyst in Brazil; no affiliation with VITAL.
- CASE-006: no poster was displayed at all.
- CASE-007: no poster was displayed either; the posting was made under the company account itself, so the unrelated-poster indicator is absent rather than present.
- CASE-001 and CASE-004: poster affiliation was not verified.

### Dead or absent domains and websites

- CASE-002: the domain nlaaa.nl does not resolve; the real organisation's official site lists no vacancies.
- CASE-005: the contact domain vital-soluciones.com does not resolve; the real firm's site has no careers page.
- CASE-006: the company page lists no website at all.
- CASE-003: no careers presence for the advertised line of business.

### Rapid closure and displayed applicant counts

Displayed applicant counts are LinkedIn's shown numbers. They do not prove that applications were received in full, or that any applicant information was used.

- CASE-002: closed approximately 2 hours after going live; 16 applicants displayed at first sighting.
- CASE-003: closed; LinkedIn displayed 26 applicants. The closure interval is Not Verified.
- CASE-006: closed within approximately 7 hours; LinkedIn displayed 26 applicants.
- CASES 001, 004, 005: applicant counts Not Verified; closure timing Not Verified for 004 and 005.

### No corroboration found outside LinkedIn

In Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006, the advertised role could not be found on the real employer's website, careers page, or anywhere else on the web. Where a real business existed behind the name, its own LinkedIn Jobs tab was empty or held only the suspicious posting itself. Case-004's role likewise had no corroboration outside the LinkedIn posting and the firm's own site. Case-007's role likewise had no corroboration anywhere — exact-title and exact-phrase searches returned zero results, and the role could not be confirmed on the firm's own website, where listings sit behind a portal login. Case-008's role likewise had no corroboration anywhere — and, uniquely in this register, neither did the employer: no company page, website, registration, or any other trace of the business was found. Case-001 is the exception that proves the need for precision: the genuine role did exist — at the real employer, EaglePicher Technologies — and only the copy appeared on LinkedIn, under a false company name. Case-011 extends this check beyond LinkedIn: the advertised role could not be found on the employer's own careers board, which was read in full, and the listing itself existed only as an aggregator card.

## Case-007: a second, distinct pattern

Case-007 (Nexorant LLC, investigated 2026-10-17) is deliberately not folded into the table above, because it does not share the table's defining indicators. It shares two of the weaker ones: a generic template that names no end employer (only "client environments"), and zero corroboration for the advertised role anywhere, including on the firm's own website. It does **not** share the impersonation indicators: no third-party company's name or page was used (the account appears to be the firm's own, with branding consistent since 2022, its own website, and a modest multi-platform footprint), no poster was displayed at all so no poster affiliation mismatch exists, no copied job text was identified, and no contact domain was dead.

The register therefore separates two distinct patterns rather than one:

1. **Impersonation of third-party companies** — Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006, where real foreign businesses' names or pages carried roles those businesses have no connection to (with Case-001 a copying variant: a real employer's job text reposted under a false advertised identity).
2. **Unverifiable blind staffing posts** — Cases 004 and 007, where a traceable firm posts a role naming no end employer, the role cannot be corroborated anywhere, and the evidence supports "not verified — skip" but not "fake".

Keeping the two patterns separate matters: the second pattern can describe legitimate-but-opaque staffing behaviour as well as abuse, which is exactly why Cases 004 and 007 carry the weaker verdict.

## Case-008: a third variant — fabricated employer identity

Case-008 (REVO — Revolutionary Volatility Operations, investigated 2026-10-20) shares the register's core weak indicators with both patterns above — a generic template description naming no verifiable specific, and zero corroboration for the advertised role anywhere — but it matches neither pattern's identity mechanism. No third-party company was impersonated, because no business under the advertised name could be found at all: no LinkedIn company page exists (the name on the posting is non-clickable text), no website exists, no business registration under the name was located, and an exact-name web search returned zero results about any such company. Nor is it a blind staffing post, because there is no traceable firm doing the posting.

The register therefore records a third variant alongside the two patterns:

3. **Fabricated employer identity** — Case-008, where the advertised employer itself appears invented wholesale: a template US-remote cybersecurity posting with no company page, no named poster, no website, no registration, and no footprint of any kind behind it.

The distinction matters for the same reason the two patterns are kept separate: impersonation leaves a real victim business behind it; fabrication, on the evidence available, leaves none that could be identified. Who created the Case-008 posting, and for what purpose, is Not Verified.

## Case-009: a fourth variant — stale / recirculating requisition

Case-009 (Wrench Group, investigated 2026-10-23) matches none of the three patterns above, and it is deliberately not folded into any of them. The employer is real, the posting ran under the employer's own name, its website and contact domain resolve, and no impersonation, fabrication, or blind-staffing indicator was established. The single decisive finding is narrower: at verification time the employer's own iCIMS applicant tracking system carried no such opening at all — an unfiltered search showed only an unrelated HVAC Maintenance Technician job, and keyword searches and the IT category filter all returned "no jobs found" — while the listing text matches an approximately 8-month-old mirror under a different title ("Cyber Fusion Analyst") and one mirror already records the role as no longer listed.

The register therefore records a fourth variant:

4. **Stale / recirculating requisition** — Case-009, where a role advertised under a real employer's own name cannot be confirmed as a live opening on the employer's own system, and the documented evidence points to an old requisition recirculating through LinkedIn and aggregators rather than to a fresh opening.

The distinction matters: a stale requisition can be a platform or sync failure rather than anyone's deception, which is exactly why Case-009 carries the "not verified — skip" verdict and the employer is not labelled fraudulent. The practical lesson is the same one the whole register runs on: the employer's own ATS is the controlling source, and a listing that exists only in aggregator mirrors has not been verified.

## Case-010: the impersonation indicator cluster, recorded not verified

Case-010 (Spiralation, investigated 2026-10-23) is not folded into the impersonation pattern above, even though it carries that pattern's indicator cluster almost in full: a real foreign business unconnected to the advertised role (a Sri Lankan government startup-accelerator program run under ICTA Sri Lanka, Colombo, with no US presence and no security-sector activity), a poster publicly affiliated elsewhere (an ETL/Informatica data-engineering developer, not an HR or recruiting account, with no Spiralation affiliation), a dead listed domain (spiralation.com, the website on the company page, does not resolve), and no corroboration for the role outside LinkedIn (the program has no jobs portal, and no careers page or ATS carries the posting). Its company-page fields (industry "Fundraising", Government Agency type, HQ Colombo, 1 employee listed) contradict the advertised Michigan role in the same way the patterned cases' pages contradicted theirs.

It is recorded **not verified, not likely fake**, on the investigator's verdict, and this analysis does not upgrade it. The distinction the register maintains is between an indicator cluster and a proven mechanism: the Case-010 evidence establishes a total employer/job mismatch and the absence of any legitimate US hiring employer behind the posting, but it does not establish who created the posting or for what purpose. Case-010 therefore stands as the register's clearest demonstration that the weaker verdict is a grading decision, not an evidence gap that stronger wording could paper over: the cluster justified the skip on its own, without needing the fake label.

## Case-011: the stale / unverifiable-requisition variant, on an aggregator

Case-011 (DISCO, investigated 2026-10-24) is a second instance of the fourth variant recorded for Case-009, and it is deliberately not folded into any of the other patterns. The employer is real, the listing ran under the employer's own name, its website and careers board resolve, and no impersonation, fabrication, or blind-staffing indicator was established. The single decisive finding matches Case-009's: at verification time the employer's own careers board carried no such opening at all — csdisco.com/careers was read in full across every department, and no "Security Engineer", and no security-titled role of any kind, appears on it. DISCO's standalone Greenhouse board is deactivated, so the embedded board that was checked is the only official board where the role could appear.

Two features extend the variant without changing its verdict. First, the sighting came from an aggregator card (RemoteRocketship) rather than LinkedIn — Case-011 is the first case in this register whose original sighting was not a LinkedIn posting. Second, the card's full description sat behind a sign-up wall, so unlike Case-009, whose listing text could be read and matched against an older mirror, nothing about the advertised role beyond its title, remote claim, and undisclosed pay could be read at all. Whether the card was stale, mislabeled, or pointed to a posting that never was a live DISCO requisition is Not Verified. The distinction from Case-009 matters for the same reason Case-009's did: an aggregator card with no live requisition behind it can be a sync or listing failure rather than anyone's deception, which is exactly why Case-011 carries the "not verified — skip" verdict and DISCO is not labelled fraudulent.

## Cases 012 and 014: the employer-board-absence variant, again

Case-012 (V2Soft, investigated 2026-10-25) and Case-014 (Blue Mantis, investigated 2026-10-25) are further instances of the fourth variant recorded for Case-009 and Case-011, and they are deliberately not folded into any of the other patterns. In both, the decisive finding matches Case-009's and Case-011's: at verification time there was no such opening on the poster's or employer's own careers board. V2Soft's board listed 150 jobs with no Network Security Engineer anywhere on it (and a "CCNP" search returned zero jobs); Blue Mantis's board listed 37 jobs with no Security Analyst L1. In both cases the LinkedIn posting itself had also already vanished — invalid or removed at dig time — so unlike Case-009, whose listing text could be read and matched against an older mirror, no posting text survives in either case to verify anything against.

The two cases differ from Case-009 and from each other in ways that do not change the verdict. Case-012's poster is a staffing firm and the posting named no client employer at all, so it also carries a blind-staffing surface feature — but unlike Cases 004 and 007, whose postings stayed live and readable, the role here could not be corroborated on the firm's own board, which is the board-absence finding, and the client, the certification labels, and the direct-vs-contract question are all unknowable from a dead posting. Case-014's listing additionally contradicted itself on location (a Remote tag against body passages pointing to Portsmouth, NH and Woodbridge, NJ) and blended L1 SOC duties with IT-support work — sloppiness indicators, recorded as documented, that still do not establish who posted the role or why. In neither case was an impersonation, fabrication, or dead-domain indicator established, and neither firm is labelled fraudulent: a role filled and removed before verification, or a board sync failure, cannot be excluded on public evidence, which is exactly why both carry the "not verified — skip" verdict.

## Cases 013 and 015: a fifth variant — mislabeled repost

Case-013 (Torentify / Innodata, investigated 2026-10-25) and Case-015 (TaskVerse / FetchJobs, investigated 2026-10-25) match none of the four variants above, because the mechanism is different in kind: in both, the poster is a third party that republishes a real employer's job under its own name, and the repost itself misrepresents the work. Case-013's poster is a small job portal in Indore, India (2–10 people) that reposts other companies' jobs under its own name, with no partnership with the named employer found anywhere; its card advertised a full-time, 100% remote cybersecurity role, while the employer's own posting for the matching work describes part-time, flexible, project-based AI-training contributor work at $15/hr — rating model outputs, labeling, ranking, and rewriting. Case-015's poster is a storefront for a job-board aggregator; its body is a stripped-down paraphrase of a real CrowdStrike Systems Engineer II role (R29877, $100,000–$145,000, 3+ years required) with exactly the specifics an applicant would judge the role by — the experience requirement and the pay — removed, and the duties are production-systems / SRE work rather than cybersecurity analysis.

The register therefore records a fifth variant alongside the four above:

5. **Mislabeled repost** — Cases 013 and 015, where a third-party front republishes a real employer's job under its own name and the repost misrepresents the work: a full-time cybersecurity label over part-time gig AI-training work (Case-013), or a real role's text paraphrased with its requirements and pay stripped (Case-015).

The distinction matters, and it is why the verdict is "not verified — mislabeled repost" rather than "fake". Unlike the impersonation pattern, no real business's page or identity was hijacked on the documented evidence, and unlike the fabricated-identity variant, the underlying employers and their real postings are identifiable (Innodata Inc.; CrowdStrike R29877). What is established is the mislabeling itself and the absence of any evidence that either repost was authorized — authorization is Not Verified in both cases. What the reposters gain (traffic, signups, applicant flow) is a hypothesis, recorded as such in the case files, not an established finding. A deliberate control confirms the grading discipline: a second Torentify card the same day pointed at a real CACI role that failed only on a required 5+ years of experience; it was excluded from this register entirely, because a rule-fail on a real job is not a case, and logging it would mislabel a real employer.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

The evidence supports a shared pattern and playbook across Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006: real foreign business names or pages, generic US-remote cybersecurity templates, posters with no affiliation to the named company, dead or absent domains, and no corroboration for the roles outside LinkedIn. On seniority, the precise record is: most of these postings were junior or entry-titled (Cases 003, 005, 006), while Case-002 was open to 0–3 years of experience. On closure, the documented record is narrower than a blanket claim: Case-002 closed approximately 2 hours after going live and Case-006 within approximately 7 hours; Case-003 also closed, with its interval Not Verified, and Case-005's closure status is Not Verified. Case-001 used a different technique — copying a real employer's job text and reposting it under a false advertised identity — and its copied body text was senior-level, not entry-level. Why any of these postings was created (for example, to harvest applicant information) is a hypothesis, not an established finding. Case-004 shares only the weakest indicators (no corroboration found for the role, identity mismatch) and remains unverifiable rather than fake. Case-007, added 2026-10-17, shares the blind-posting and zero-role-corroboration indicators but not the impersonation indicators, and joins Case-004 in the second, distinct category of unverifiable blind staffing posts (see "Case-007: a second, distinct pattern" above).

The evidence does **not** establish that one single actor runs all of these postings. Template reuse and similar timing are consistent with a shared playbook, copied techniques, or the same actor, and the available public evidence cannot distinguish between those possibilities. **No attribution claim is made.**
