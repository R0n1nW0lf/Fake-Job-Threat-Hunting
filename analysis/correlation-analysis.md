# Correlation Analysis

This analysis compares the eight investigated cases to identify shared indicators, reused material, and common patterns. It separates what the evidence shows from what it does not.

## Shared patterns

### Reused templates and sister postings

- CASE-002: a sibling posting ("Operations Analyst", New York, LinkedIn 4478159278) ran under the same company name using an identical template.
- CASE-003: the same company page ran a second template posting ("IT Service Desk Analyst") with a different, also unrelated, poster.
- CASE-005: a template-identical sister posting ("Data Insights Analyst (Entry / Junior)", New York, 2026-09-07) ran under the same company name.
- CASE-006: the description was a generic template naming no employer at all, and exact-phrase searches for its distinctive lines returned zero results anywhere.
- CASE-001: the posting text was copied from a real employer's job (EaglePicher Technologies), and later reposts under the same name copied other companies' text (Telos in the 2026-10-04 repost; a MANTECH role in the 2026-10-09 "Senior Cyber Security Analyst" repost).

### Company-page impersonation and placeholder display names

- CASE-006 is the clearest example of an identity inconsistency: a real Mexican law firm's LinkedIn page displays the placeholder name "No aplica", while its URL slug still names the firm. A second, unrelated company's page (slug "catelca", Panama) displays the same placeholder name. **Whether either page was renamed, by whom, or without authorization is Not Verified** — the confirmed observation is the placeholder display and the name/slug mismatch, not the mechanism behind it.
- CASE-002 and CASE-005 used the names of real foreign organisations (a Dutch coalition; a Costa Rican outsourcing firm) that have no connection to the advertised roles.
- CASE-003's page belongs to a real but unrelated Dutch micro-business.

### Poster affiliation mismatches

- CASE-002: poster publicly a T-Mobile program manager; no affiliation with NL AAA.
- CASE-003: poster an unrelated person in Argentina, in an unrelated profession; a second posting on the same page used yet another unrelated poster.
- CASE-005: poster publicly a Tata Consultancy Services support analyst in Brazil; no affiliation with VITAL.
- CASE-006: no poster was displayed at all.
- CASE-007: no poster was displayed either; the posting was made under the company account itself, so the unrelated-poster indicator is absent rather than present.
- CASE-001 and CASE-004: poster affiliation was not verified.

### Dead or absent domains and websites

- CASE-002: the domain nlaaa.nl does not resolve; the real organisation's official site lists no vacancies.
- CASE-005: the contact domain vital-soluciones.com does not resolve; the real firm's site has no careers page.
- CASE-006: the company page lists no website at all.
- CASE-003: no careers presence for the advertised line of business.

### Rapid closure and displayed applicant counts

Displayed applicant counts are LinkedIn's shown numbers. They do not prove that applications were received in full, or that any applicant information was used.

- CASE-002: closed approximately 2 hours after going live; 16 applicants displayed at first sighting.
- CASE-003: closed; LinkedIn displayed 26 applicants. The closure interval is Not Verified.
- CASE-006: closed within approximately 7 hours; LinkedIn displayed 26 applicants.
- CASES 001, 004, 005: applicant counts Not Verified; closure timing Not Verified for 004 and 005.

### No corroboration found outside LinkedIn

In Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006, the advertised role could not be found on the real employer's website, careers page, or anywhere else on the web. Where a real business existed behind the name, its own LinkedIn Jobs tab was empty or held only the suspicious posting itself. Case-004's role likewise had no corroboration outside the LinkedIn posting and the firm's own site. Case-007's role likewise had no corroboration anywhere — exact-title and exact-phrase searches returned zero results, and the role could not be confirmed on the firm's own website, where listings sit behind a portal login. Case-008's role likewise had no corroboration anywhere — and, uniquely in this register, neither did the employer: no company page, website, registration, or any other trace of the business was found. Case-001 is the exception that proves the need for precision: the genuine role did exist — at the real employer, EaglePicher Technologies — and only the copy appeared on LinkedIn, under a false company name.

## Case-007: a second, distinct pattern

Case-007 (Nexorant LLC, investigated 2026-10-17) is deliberately not folded into the table above, because it does not share the table's defining indicators. It shares two of the weaker ones: a generic template that names no end employer (only "client environments"), and zero corroboration for the advertised role anywhere, including on the firm's own website. It does **not** share the impersonation indicators: no third-party company's name or page was used (the account appears to be the firm's own, with branding consistent since 2022, its own website, and a modest multi-platform footprint), no poster was displayed at all so no poster affiliation mismatch exists, no copied job text was identified, and no contact domain was dead.

The register therefore separates two distinct patterns rather than one:

1. **Impersonation of third-party companies** — Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006, where real foreign businesses' names or pages carried roles those businesses have no connection to (with Case-001 a copying variant: a real employer's job text reposted under a false advertised identity).
2. **Unverifiable blind staffing posts** — Cases 004 and 007, where a traceable firm posts a role naming no end employer, the role cannot be corroborated anywhere, and the evidence supports "not verified — skip" but not "fake".

Keeping the two patterns separate matters: the second pattern can describe legitimate-but-opaque staffing behaviour as well as abuse, which is exactly why Cases 004 and 007 carry the weaker verdict.

## Case-008: a third variant — fabricated employer identity

Case-008 (REVO — Revolutionary Volatility Operations, investigated 2026-10-20) shares the register's core weak indicators with both patterns above — a generic template description naming no verifiable specific, and zero corroboration for the advertised role anywhere — but it matches neither pattern's identity mechanism. No third-party company was impersonated, because no business under the advertised name could be found at all: no LinkedIn company page exists (the name on the posting is non-clickable text), no website exists, no business registration under the name was located, and an exact-name web search returned zero results about any such company. Nor is it a blind staffing post, because there is no traceable firm doing the posting.

The register therefore records a third variant alongside the two patterns:

3. **Fabricated employer identity** — Case-008, where the advertised employer itself appears invented wholesale: a template US-remote cybersecurity posting with no company page, no named poster, no website, no registration, and no footprint of any kind behind it.

The distinction matters for the same reason the two patterns are kept separate: impersonation leaves a real victim business behind it; fabrication, on the evidence available, leaves none that could be identified. Who created the Case-008 posting, and for what purpose, is Not Verified.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

The evidence supports a shared pattern and playbook across Cases 002, 003, 005, and 006: real foreign business names or pages, generic US-remote cybersecurity templates, posters with no affiliation to the named company, dead or absent domains, and no corroboration for the roles outside LinkedIn. On seniority, the precise record is: most of these postings were junior or entry-titled (Cases 003, 005, 006), while Case-002 was open to 0–3 years of experience. On closure, the documented record is narrower than a blanket claim: Case-002 closed approximately 2 hours after going live and Case-006 within approximately 7 hours; Case-003 also closed, with its interval Not Verified, and Case-005's closure status is Not Verified. Case-001 used a different technique — copying a real employer's job text and reposting it under a false advertised identity — and its copied body text was senior-level, not entry-level. Why any of these postings was created (for example, to harvest applicant information) is a hypothesis, not an established finding. Case-004 shares only the weakest indicators (no corroboration found for the role, identity mismatch) and remains unverifiable rather than fake. Case-007, added 2026-10-17, shares the blind-posting and zero-role-corroboration indicators but not the impersonation indicators, and joins Case-004 in the second, distinct category of unverifiable blind staffing posts (see "Case-007: a second, distinct pattern" above).

The evidence does **not** establish that one single actor runs all of these postings. Template reuse and similar timing are consistent with a shared playbook, copied techniques, or the same actor, and the available public evidence cannot distinguish between those possibilities. **No attribution claim is made.**
